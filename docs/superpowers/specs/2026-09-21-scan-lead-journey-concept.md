The scan must show the actual work we propose to create for this client. The moving person guides the reader between those samples. Each sample uses the client's business, audience and topic. RISE examples provide supporting proof; the client still gets their own proposed materials.

Required samples and their place in the story:

The newsletter needs its own subject, useful content and relevant next step. Show the resource delivery email separately when needed. Joining the newsletter is an explicit choice in the example; a resource request alone does not imply newsletter subscription.

Page copy should use short, everyday words. Aim for a heading of roughly 4–8 words and one explanation of roughly 12–20 words at each stop. These are editing guides, not reasons to cut a necessary explanation. No stacked paragraphs explaining the same stage twice. Avoid terms such as nurturing, qualification and attribution in customer-facing chapter titles; say “Stay in touch”, “See if you can help” and “See where they came from”.

The sample itself remains complete and properly formatted. The short-copy target applies to the surrounding explanation. Allow a real post or newsletter to take the space it needs. The reader should see one main sample at a time, with normal scrolling and simple carousel controls. Essential samples must not open into a separate detailed dashboard.

## Presentation scope and shared showcase, latest clarification

> i mean the carousel we already do it but its the presentation that needs to be more interesting.. carousel and lead magnets can belong sameish section

Use the existing carousel and lead-magnet outputs as the material. The design task concerns their presentation within the scan: scale, arrangement, movement, reading order and the connection to the buyer's next action. Redesigning the carousel content or its production process is outside this concept's scope unless separately requested.

Group the carousel and lead magnet into one continuous showcase. The carousel introduces a useful idea; the resource gives the buyer a way to apply it. Retain both formats and their distinct roles within that section.

Suggested presentation: the existing carousel appears as a large readable deck. As the visitor scrolls onward, the next related resource comes into view, with the same buyer and topic carried across. Both remain accessible inline. A short connecting sentence explains why someone would move from reading the idea to using the resource. Do not imply a carousel always requires a lead magnet or invent a connection between unrelated existing samples.

The showcase is one stop in the wider lead journey. Newsletter and follow-up continue after the resource request. The discovery post and profile establish how the buyer arrived here. Existing references to a standalone carousel composition describe a beat within this shared section, not a separate mandatory chapter.

## Recommendation

A scroll-driven illustrated lead moves through recognisable LinkedIn surfaces. The person represents one hypothetical buyer of the prospect's service. For Andrew, that could be a brand marketing lead preparing a coffee campaign.

Normal scrolling moves the person and the scene. At each stop, the person pauses while the artifact remains readable. The visitor follows one continuous example with stable buyer, category, purpose and source context.

The working title is “Follow your next potential client.” Final copy should avoid implying every visitor becomes a client.

## How outreach joins

There are two entrances to the same overall process:

- Inbound entrance: relevant post → profile → resource request.
- Researched outreach entrance: relevant message → profile or resource → response.

Default to the inbound example. A simple route choice can replay the opening with a researched message. The message must show its reason for relevance. Any bracketed project reference remains visibly unverified until source material is available.

Both entrances converge into the common resource, context and follow-up scenes. Explain that some prospects reply directly to outreach; they do not have to download anything first. Do not teach a rigid funnel as the only possible behaviour.

## The final visual reveal

At the end, pull back enough to show the route the reader just followed. Keep the same buyer visible. Show the source, resource choice and resulting conversation together.

The intended takeaway: “I understand where the buyer comes from, why they respond, and what you would run for me.”

The overview is a recap of visible artifacts. Avoid introducing a new system diagram or extra claims at this point.

## Scene contract

Every scene needs all of the following before it is designed:

1. A specific buyer situation carried from the preceding scene.
2. One short line explaining why this stage belongs in the strategy.
3. The full main artifact, readable without opening a panel.
4. A visible action or change that justifies movement to the next scene.
5. A short statement of what we would operate for the prospect, when that is not apparent from the artifact.

A scene that can be moved anywhere without changing the meaning needs a stronger connection to its neighbours.

## Interaction and reading

- Scroll is the primary control. No keyboard game controls, timed challenges or required exploration.
- Keep headings large, supporting explanations short and the reading direction vertical.
- Posts, messages and resource outputs are HTML content, not text baked into backgrounds.
- Essential explanations stay on the main page. Optional clicks change the example, navigate a carousel or try the resource.
- A full post can occupy more than one screen. Character movement pauses while the reader scrolls through the post; text does not race away.
- Scene changes use normal scroll position. Avoid repeated long pinned stretches with little visible progress.
- On mobile, give the artifact the width it needs. The character can travel through section transitions and rest beside a chapter marker.
- Reduced-motion mode shows the same ordered story and complete artifacts with static character positions.
- Music remains an optional experiment and starts off. The narrative must make sense in silence.

## Art direction, clarified by Ivan

> yeah and each one should feel like fucking art when u read it.... u can find inspirations in 21st dev or just be creative... using super simple language

The composition should make each piece feel deliberately made for this client. Keep the language direct and easy to follow. The common character, path and recurring resource connect scenes with different layouts.

### Composition by sample

| Piece | Composition and movement | Reading rule |
|---|---|---|
| Post | The surrounding feed recedes as one complete post comes into view. The character stops beside it. Use real paragraph spacing, a visible author and an attachment that belongs to the subject. | The whole post is available inline. Movement settles before the reader starts. |
| Carousel | Present the designed slide as the focal point, with the edge of the next slide suggesting a deck. A controlled slide change reveals the next idea. Each slide gets a composition suited to its content. | One idea per slide. Swipe and labelled arrows both work. The title or argument stays legible during transitions. |
| Profile | Let the post's author identity carry into the profile scene. Positioning and featured work give the buyer a reason to continue to the resource. | Show the relevant profile material at readable scale. Avoid a tiny screenshot of an entire profile. |
| Lead magnet | Present an actual tool, guide or worksheet with its own client identity. For CueVu, let the selected research question become a visible brief. | The result is immediately understandable. One short line explains what the buyer can do with it. |
| Newsletter | A carefully typeset email with a subject, a strong opening, useful content and one next step. The resource or topic from the previous scene reappears naturally. | Show a complete sample issue, with a generous reading column and ordinary scrolling. |
| Outreach and reply | The same person's context travels into a recognisable message thread. Show the message in full, with enough space around it to read comfortably. | Each message makes sense from the prior scene. Label hypothetical replies clearly. |

The signature transition is a visible hand-off: the resource offered in the post becomes the tool they use, then the item delivered to their inbox. This carries meaning between scenes. The walking character follows those actions.

Use the product's white, ink and restrained red for the surrounding story. LinkedIn and email artifacts retain recognisable platform styling. Client resources retain the client's identity. Choose and settle one character style before generating a pose set.

A single main action should carry each transition. Keep the rest of the scene still while people read. Decorative motion, sound and background treatments get added only where the storyboard has a clear place for them.

### Review standard

Review each scene at its real reading size before adding animation. It must identify the client, show a complete usable sample and explain its purpose in a short line. Then review the hand-off into the next scene. Reject repeated card layouts, headline-only samples, unreadable screenshot text and transitions that conceal the content.

## Artwork and implementation direction, provisional

Use the existing React and motion stack for the first prototype. Native HTML handles text and interactions; an SVG route and a small illustrated character handle movement. This allows keyboard navigation, text selection and responsive reflow.

A game engine is unnecessary for the proposed scroll interaction. Reassess only if Ivan chooses actual player control or collision-based gameplay later.

Character art needs a deliberate style decision. Build one consistent character with idle and walking poses, rather than independently generated figures for every scene. AI-generated backgrounds can be explored after the scene composition is settled. LinkedIn screens and client proof remain actual interface content.

## Planning and validation sequence

1. Choose the visual direction and refine the six-scene storyboard.
2. Draw a static strip with the actual example copy and artifacts. Check the story without animation.
3. Prototype only discovery → profile → resource to establish the character, camera and reading behaviour.
4. Review the prototype at desktop and phone sizes before extending the remaining scenes.
5. Add the request, complete newsletter sample, follow-up and outcome overview, keeping context consistent.
6. Validate reading, optional controls, reduced motion and truthful state labels. Then consider music and background treatments.

Acceptance questions for the storyboard and prototype:

- Can a first-time viewer explain why this buyer would care about the post?
- Can they explain why the resource is worth requesting?
- Do they know what CueVu learns from the request?
- Can they distinguish delivery, nurture, researched outreach and project qualification?
- Can they follow the same person's context all the way through?
- Does the page remain useful if they ignore every optional control?
- Are the complete posts, carousel, lead magnet, newsletter and messages present and readable without opening detailed panels?
- Can the reader explain each stage from its heading, short explanation and sample?
- Does every main sample belong to this client and the same buyer problem?
